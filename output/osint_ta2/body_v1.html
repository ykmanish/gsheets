Before you read

Analyst's note

This dossier looks at three oil tankers that were handed to me as nothing more than IMO numbers: 9384306, 9236248 and 9259197. The brief was simple to state and harder to do well: work out who these ships really are today, what they have been called before, who stands behind them, whether they are sanctioned, and whether their tracking data behaves the way an honest merchant ship's should.

Everything here was collected passively. I did not contact the vessels, their owners or their agents, I did not create accounts or pay for commercial AIS feeds, and I did not try to get around any website that refused automated or anonymous access. Where a source blocked me (the Ukrainian GUR "War & Sanctions" portal did, with a bot-check page), I used a reputable aggregator that republishes the same data instead, and I say so wherever it matters. All screenshots were taken on 26 November 2026 and the satellite frames come from the European Space Agency's free Copernicus archive.

A word on tone. Shadow-fleet reporting has a habit of treating every gap in a ship's AIS track as proof of wrongdoing. I have tried not to do that. Each vessel section ends with the most reasonable innocent explanations I could think of, and I tested each one against what the evidence actually shows. Some survive in part. Most do not.

Bottom line up front

The three ships at a glance

All three hulls are older tankers that changed name and flag several times as they aged, and all three now sit on the sanctions lists of the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Ukraine. They are not, however, the same kind of problem. One is a Russian product tanker that has simply come home to the Russian flag. One is an Iranian-trade crude carrier whose tracking identity does not add up. The third has worked for Russia, Iran and Venezuela in turn and tried to slip out of a naval blockade with its transponder switched off.

What ties them together

None of the three share an owner, a manager or a flag that I could find, so I would not describe them as one network. What they share is a pattern. Each was a perfectly ordinary mainstream tanker for most of its life. Each was sold on in its late teens, given a new name, and moved to a registry with light oversight. Each then started carrying cargo from a sanctioned oil exporter. And in each case the paper identity (flag, MMSI, owner) became less stable just as sanctions pressure arrived. That is the textbook life cycle of a shadow-fleet tanker, and it is the main reason my confidence is high across the board even where specific ownership details remain hidden.

Vessel 1 of 3

UNIVERSAL

Chemical / oil products tanker · built 2009 · LOA 183 m × 32.2 m · GT 29,967
IMO9384306MMSI 273271630 · UAPD7

Intelligence assessment

High confidence UNIVERSAL is the former Sovcomflot product tanker SCF USSURI. It was given a neutral name in September 2024, passed through a string of open registries, and is now back under the Russian flag with a Russian owner and manager, North Fleet Ltd. Six governments have sanctioned it for carrying Russian oil above the price cap, and Ukraine has also sanctioned its master. Its recent behaviour fits that profile. Its known calls are at Ust-Luga, Suez, Port Said and, unusually, Manaus in Brazil. It went quiet on terrestrial AIS off northern Russia about six weeks ago while declaring Ust-Luga as its destination. Press reports describe it being escorted by a Russian warship and drifting for long periods at sea. I rate this high because the identity chain (IMO, former names, MMSIs and call signs) agrees across government lists, OpenSanctions, VesselFinder and MagicPort, and because no source I found points to legitimate, non-sanctioned employment. The weaker part is what the ship is doing right now. That rests on a 43-day-old terrestrial fix and on paywalled satellite AIS, so I would put its current activity at moderate.

Competing hypotheses

H1 · The AIS gap is a coverage problem, not concealment Partly consistent

Terrestrial AIS receivers are thin along the Russian Arctic coast, and the free VesselFinder view shows only terrestrial fixes. A ship on the Northern Sea Route or in the Barents Sea can disappear from free maps for weeks without switching anything off. Test: this explains the current 43-day gap reasonably well. It does not explain GUR's specific finding that the ship ran Baltic exports with AIS disabled, since the Baltic is one of the best-covered seas in the world.

H2 · The reflag to Russia is a lawful commercial response to sanctions Partly consistent

Once a ship is designated, most open registries de-flag it, and insurers and banks pull out. A Russian owner moving its ship to the Russian flag is legal and, arguably, more transparent than staying on a flag of convenience. Test: this fits the most recent move. It does not explain the earlier move from Sovcomflot branding to a neutral name and UAE-registered single-ship managers in 2024, which happened before any designation and looks like distancing.

H3 · The Manaus call and Atlantic drift were ordinary tramp trading Weakly consistent

Brazil legally buys large volumes of Russian diesel, and tankers often drift while waiting for orders. Test: the Brazil leg on its own is plausible. Taken with the reported warship escort, the aborted Cuba voyage and the long unexplained interval since July, it reads more like a ship working for state-linked cargo interests than a normal tramp.

Net judgement: the benign explanations account for individual data points, but not for the overall pattern. The sanctioned, shadow-fleet assessment stands at high confidence.

Vessel 2 of 3

FENGHUANG

Crude oil tanker (Aframax) · built 2002 · LOA 248 m × 43 m · GT 59,693
IMO9236248AIS MMSI 650000127 · C9NA78

Intelligence assessment

High confidence FENGHUANG is the 2002-built Aframax that traded for 18 years as MINERVA ZENIA in a mainstream Greek fleet. It is now an Iran-trade shadow tanker. The US designated it on 24 February 2025 as property of Alkonost Maritime DMCC, a Dubai company sanctioned for moving Iranian oil, and within a month it was renamed FENGHUANG ("phoenix" in Chinese, after its earlier name PHOENIX I). It has since been sanctioned by Ukraine, the EU, Switzerland and the UK. Its identity is now visibly inconsistent. It transmits a Mozambique MMSI (650000127) and Mozambique call sign, while OpenSanctions records a Cook Islands flag and VesselFinder's particulars say Comoros. Three different flag states for one hull at the same time is a strong sign of a fraudulent or lapsed registration. Its recent pattern of no declared destination ("FOR ORDER"), deep laden draught and calls at Huizhou (China) and Pengerang (Malaysia) matches the Iran-to-China supply chain through Malaysian ship-to-ship transfer areas. The identity and sanctions picture is high confidence. Who actually owns the ship today is only moderate, because the registered owner is hidden behind commercial paywalls and the OFAC notice names only the company with an "interest" in the ship.

Competing hypotheses

H1 · The flag/MMSI mismatch is just database lag after a genuine re-registration Partly consistent

Commercial databases update flags at different speeds, and a ship that really did move to Mozambique could still show Comoros or Cook Islands elsewhere for months. Test: lag can explain two sources disagreeing. It is a harder fit for three flags at once, and I found no public record of a Mozambique registration. The Cook Islands registry has said it is removing sanctioned tankers, which would explain why that flag lapsed but not where the ship went legitimately.

H2 · "FOR ORDER" and slow steaming are normal tramp practice Partly consistent

Many legitimate tankers sail "for orders" while the charterer finds a buyer, and slow steaming saves fuel. Test: true in general, but a laden, sanctioned crude carrier waiting near Malaysian transfer anchorages with no stated buyer is exactly how Iranian barrels are moved into China. The behaviour is normal; the context is not.

H3 · The renames were part of an ordinary sale chain Weakly consistent

Old tankers often change hands and names. Test: the 2020 sale does look like an ordinary end-of-life sale. The March 2025 rename, a few weeks after the OFAC action, does not; its timing points to evading sanctions screening.

Net judgement: no benign explanation accounts for the three-flag conflict or the timing of the post-designation rename. Assessment held at high confidence.

THALIA III

Crude oil tanker (Aframax) · built 2003 · LOA 246.8 m × 42 m · GT 57,979
IMO9259197MMSI 352002137 · 3E3812

Intelligence assessment

High confidence THALIA III is a 2003 Aframax, built as PACIFIC LEO, that has become one of the more versatile shadow-fleet tankers. Sanctions narratives tie it to Russian crude from the Baltic, Black Sea and Pacific (Kozmino) going to China and India, and to Iranian and Venezuelan oil. In early January 2026 it tried to leave Venezuelan waters with AIS switched off to get past the US naval blockade. It turned back, loaded with PDVSA crude, after the US detained the tanker OLINA on 9 January. VesselFinder has shown its flag as "Unknown" since January 2025, the month OFAC designated it, which strongly suggests the Panamanian registration was withdrawn. It is now on the US, EU, Swiss, UK, New Zealand, Canadian and Ukrainian lists, and Ukraine has also sanctioned its master. Confidence in the identity, sanctions status and dark activity is high. Confidence in current ownership is moderate: one press source names Selena Lively Limited as the registered owner at the time of the US action, but I could not confirm the present owner or manager from open records.

Competing hypotheses

H1 · "Unknown" flag is a database gap, not a lost registration Partly consistent

Free AIS sites sometimes blank fields when a ship re-registers and its new flag has not been processed. Test: a gap of nearly two years is far too long for processing lag, and it starts in the same month as the OFAC designation. Panama's announced policy of cancelling registrations of designated ships is the simpler explanation.

H2 · Loading at José was licensed trade Weakly consistent

Some companies have operated in Venezuela under specific US licences, so a tanker at José is not automatically illicit. Test: licences of that kind cover named, non-sanctioned operators. A ship that is itself OFAC-designated and tried to leave with its AIS off is not behaving like a licensed carrier.

H3 · Malaysian calls were routine bunkering and crew changes Partly consistent

Sungai Linggi and nearby anchorages are busy, legitimate bunkering and crew-change areas used by thousands of ships. Test: entirely possible for any single call. Combined with Kozmino loadings and the China discharge pattern, an STS-transfer role is more likely.

Net judgement: the January 2026 dark-exit attempt has no benign explanation that survives the facts. Assessment held at high confidence.

Closing

Limitations, gaps and what I would do next

Ownership is the weak spot. For all three ships, the registered owner and ISM manager fields that would close the loop sit behind commercial paywalls (VesselFinder, Equasis requires an account, Lloyd's List Intelligence is subscription-only). I avoided treating the earlier student draft I was shown as a source. Where it named managers I could not verify (for example a manager for THALIA III and a second MMSI under the GUNDALA name), I have left them out rather than repeat them.

Imagery can measure, not identify. Sentinel-2's 10 m pixels are enough to tell a 180 m product tanker from a 250 m Aframax, and that is how I used them. Naming a specific ship would need sub-metre commercial imagery or a matching AIS fix at the moment of capture. Cloud cover in the Baltic in November also ruled out checking UNIVERSAL's declared 23 Nov arrival at Ust-Luga.

AIS here is terrestrial only. Free views show only terrestrial AIS, so the "days ago" figures overstate genuine silence in remote seas, especially for UNIVERSAL in the Arctic. A satellite-AIS subscription would settle whether these are blackouts or coverage gaps.

Next passive steps, if time allowed: (1) a free Equasis account to pull the ISM manager and P&I history for each IMO; (2) Sentinel-1 radar frames, which see through cloud, over Ust-Luga for 20–25 Nov; (3) the Global Fishing Watch public map, to check for AIS gaps and encounters; (4) corporate registry searches for North Fleet Ltd (Russia), Alkonost Maritime DMCC (DMCC registry) and Selena Lively Ltd.
